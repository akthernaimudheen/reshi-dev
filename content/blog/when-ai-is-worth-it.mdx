We build AI assistants. We also talk clients out of them roughly as often as we build them, which is an odd position for a company with "AI" in its name.

The reason is simple: an assistant is a solution to a specific problem, and most businesses that ask for one have a different problem.

## The four conditions

An assistant earns its cost when all four of these are true. Three out of four usually means wait.

### 1. The same questions arrive repeatedly

Look at your last two hundred enquiries. If forty percent of them are variations on five questions, that is the signal. A wedding venue we worked with was answering the same tariff, capacity and parking questions dozens of times a week — that is a machine's job.

If every enquiry is genuinely different, an assistant will handle almost none of them and you will have paid for a tool that escalates everything.

### 2. They arrive when nobody is available

Out-of-hours volume is what turns deflection into revenue rather than convenience. If ninety percent of your enquiries land between ten and six and you answer them within the hour, an assistant saves you some typing. Worth doing eventually; not worth doing first.

### 3. The answers exist somewhere authoritative

This is the condition that quietly kills most projects. An assistant grounded in a tariff sheet, a menu and a policy document is reliable. An assistant grounded in "what Rajesh usually tells people" is a liability, because nobody can tell you whether its answer was right.

If your policies live in one person's head, the first deliverable is writing them down. That has value even if you never build the assistant.

### 4. Speed of first reply decides who wins

In some businesses the first responder gets the booking. In others the customer compares carefully over two weeks and replying in ninety seconds changes nothing. Know which one you are.

## The project we talked out of it

A retail client wanted an assistant on their storefront. All four conditions failed: enquiry volume was low, questions were varied and product-specific, the product data was inconsistent across systems, and buyers were browsing rather than asking.

What they actually had was a traffic problem and a product-data problem. An assistant would have sat on a page few people reached, answering from a catalogue that was wrong in places. We fixed the data and the storefront instead. Revenue tripled over the following year without a single AI feature shipping.

That is the thing worth saying plainly: **an assistant bolted onto a site nobody visits is a traffic problem wearing an AI costume.**

## What good looks like

Where the conditions do hold, this is the shape we build:

- **Grounded in your sources**, with a pipeline that keeps them current. A stale assistant is worse than none.
- **Guardrails and a refusal path.** It should say "let me get someone" rather than improvise on pricing, medical questions, or anything contractual.
- **A human handoff with context attached.** The team receives a summary, not a transcript to read.
- **Measured weekly** on deflection rate, escalation rate and satisfaction. If deflection sits under about thirty percent after tuning, we would rather turn it off than defend it.

## The uncomfortable summary

AI is genuinely useful for a narrow, well-defined set of problems that many local businesses do have. It is also the easiest thing in the world to sell to someone who has not been told what it will not do.

Ask any agency pitching you an assistant which of the four conditions your business fails. If they cannot name one, they have not looked.
